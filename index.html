Rising Tides: A Tale of Two Futures

Chapter 01

The Global Unequal Rise

Sea level rise does not happen globally at the same level. Thermal expansion of ocean water and changes from melting ice sheets cause sea levels to vary dramatically between different parts of the world.

Chapter 02

US East Coast: Accelerated Atlantic

Cities along the US East Coast face sea level rise significantly higher than the global average. Water ends up piling up against the Atlantic coast, causing more land to get submerged.

Chapter 03

Gulf of Mexico: Rapid Subsidence

The Gulf Coast experiences the fastest relative sea level rise in North America. It also shows high differences between the two projections.

Chapter 04

US West Coast: Bluffs & Estuaries

Steep cliffs along the Pacific coastline prevent major inland flooding, but low-lying bays like the San Francisco Bay still face some risks.

Chapter 05

Hawaii: Isolated Island Vulnerability

In Hawaii, lots of infrastructure sits only a few feet above the water. Rising seas erode beaches and cause flooding.

Chapter 06

Bangladesh: The Mega-Delta Crisis

In the delta near Bangladesh, rising seas threaten tens of millions of people. Ocean water contaminates soil and drinking water, creating the potential for a humanitarian catastrophe.

Chapter 07

Pacific Atolls: Existential Ground Zero

Low elevation nations like Tuvalu and Kiribati are only two meters above current tides. Under high emissions, rising oceans will wash over entire islands, threatening these countries' existence.

Chapter 08

The Path Ahead: Divergence by 2100

The gap between large-scale climate action (SSP1-2.6) and high amounts of emissions (SSP5-8.5) represents the difference between manageable adaptation and catastrophic loss of coastal communities worldwide.

Prototype Status

What have we done so far?

We built a scrollytelling page around CESM2 sea surface height (zos) from CMIP6. The main map shows sea level change against a 1995-2014 baseline on a blue-to-red scale. Readers can switch between SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5, and between 2050 and 2100. They can also turn on a difference view that shows how much worse the high-emissions future is. An animate button flips between the two scenarios so the gap is easy to see. As you scroll, the map zooms to regions like the US coasts, Hawaii, Bangladesh, and the Pacific atolls, and the numbers below the map update for each one.
