The Uneven Nature of Sea Level Rise

Sea level rise will not happen uniformly across the globe. Explore how different emissions projections under CMIP6 models shape the future of our oceans and coastlines.

Prototype Status (Write-Up) ▼

What have we done so far?

We built a scrollytelling page around CESM2 sea surface height (zos) from CMIP6. The main map shows sea level change against a 1995-2014 baseline on a blue-to-red scale. Readers can switch between SSP1-2.6 and SSP5-8.5, and between 2050 and 2100. They can also turn on a difference view that shows how much worse the high-emissions future is. An animate button flips between the two scenarios so the gap is easy to see. As you scroll, the map zooms to regions like the US coasts, Hawaii, Bangladesh, and the Pacific atolls, and the numbers below the map update for each one.

What will be the hardest part?

We would say that our main difficulty is that we want to also add panning and zooming into the map, but the problem is how resource intensive it could be. Since our interactive visual is already a bit laggy, adding new features might make it worse. Making the website run smoother will be hard due to the large amount of data we are using, and reducing the resolution of the map would lower quality and make it look more pixelated. Therefore, we plan to try to find more efficient ways to load in and update the data before adding panning and zooming to allow the user to explore the map on their own.

Chapter 01

The Global Unequal Rise

Sea level rise does not happen globally at the same level. Thermal expansion of ocean water and changes from melting ice sheets cause sea levels to vary dramatically between different parts of the world.

Chapter 02

US East Coast

The US East Coast faces sea level rise significantly higher than the global average. Water ends up piling up against the Atlantic coast, causing more land to get submerged.

Chapter 03

Gulf of Mexico

The Gulf Coast experiences the fastest relative sea level rise in North America. It also shows high differences between the two projections, showing how its fate is closely tied with global emissions.

Chapter 04

US West Coast

The situation is not as bad along the Pacific coastline, especially since its cliffs prevent major inland flooding, but its low-lying bays like the San Francisco Bay still face some risks.

Chapter 05

Hawaii

In Hawaii, lots of infrastructure sits only a few feet above the water, making the isolated island vulnerable to sea level rise. The water near it faces relatively high sea levels, which can erode beaches and cause flooding.

Chapter 06

Bangladesh

In the delta near Bangladesh, rising seas threaten tens of millions of people. Ocean water contaminates soil and drinking water, creating the potential for a humanitarian catastrophe.

Chapter 07

Pacific Atolls

Low elevation nations like Tuvalu and Kiribati are only meters above current tides. Under high emissions, rising oceans will wash over entire islands, threatening these countries' existence.

Chapter 08

The Path Ahead: Divergence by 2100

The gap between large-scale climate action (SSP1-2.6) and high amounts of emissions (SSP5-8.5) represents the difference between being able to contain rising tides and being pulled under the waves.
